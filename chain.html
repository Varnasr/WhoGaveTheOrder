Follow the chain

Who had authority?

Authority for the use of force runs down a structure that is public, written down, and largely uncontested. Mapping it is not an accusation. It is the first honest step: before you can ask who gave an order, you have to know who could.

Two claims this interface will never merge.

Every node below carries both. The first is a statement about the law and the administrative structure. The second is a statement about this specific incident, and it requires a document. Where there is no document, the node says authorisation not established — and it will keep saying that for as long as that remains true, however obvious an inference might feel.

Why no names

There is not a single individual named anywhere in this chain, and that is a rule rather than an oversight.

A person is added to an office only when a sourced document places them in it at the relevant time. Even then, the node still reports two separate things: that they held the office, and — separately, and only if a document says so — that they authorised the action.

Naming a person because they held a post at the time is how an investigation turns into an allegation. It is also how an investigation gets dismissed.

What would change a node

Any one of these, sourced:

  • A signed deployment or dispersal order.
  • A wireless or control-room log recording an instruction and the officer who gave it.
  • A station diary or duty register entry naming the officer commanding at the time.
  • An RTI reply confirming that a record exists, and in which office it sits.
  • An affidavit or a reply in the legislature stating who authorised what.
